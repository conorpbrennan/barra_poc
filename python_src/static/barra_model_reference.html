Factor Risk Model — Data & Transformation Reference

This document is the audit trail for every number the model produces: where each input comes from, what we do to it, and which checks watch it. The book is Soros Fund Management's US equity positions, taken from their quarterly SEC 13F filings (CIK 1029160). The model is a two-block linear factor model. Portfolio P&L splits into 10 style factors plus a market intercept (the systematic block) and a diagonal name-specific block. Every scenario is the same calculation, dPnL = Σk xk · Δfk, where xk is the book's net exposure to factor k and Δfk is a shock vector. The only thing that changes between scenario types is where that shock vector comes from: historical simulation, event replay, or a hypothetical stress. All risk figures are 1-day, 99%.

Pipeline at a glance

The whole pipeline, end to end. Free public feeds go into one builder. The builder writes six parquet frames. The cube reads those frames and nothing else. The builder and the cube share no state in memory, so the six frames are the entire hand-off between data and risk.

Exposures are built monthly. The daily factor returns that drive every risk number are estimated from them in-house (§3). No factor-return series is bought or downloaded. The monthly-to-daily step is the heart of the model and is set out in §3c.

1 · Data sources

Where does the factor model come from? Read this first. The factor model is not downloaded from anyone. There is no MSCI or Barra licence and no external factor-return file behind these numbers. The 10 style factors and the market factor are built here, from scratch, in three parts:
  1. Factor definitions (the taxonomy), open-source and academic. What Size, Value, Momentum, EarnYield, Leverage, ResidVol and the rest mean follows the public literature: the Fama–French / Ken French Data Library factors, the BARRA USE4 style set, and the JKP characteristic library (Jensen–Kelly–Pedersen, jkpfactors.com). These are conventions, not a data feed. We re-implement the formulas (§3a). We do not import them.
  2. Exposures (loadings), computed here from SEC fundamentals and Stooq/Yahoo prices as cross-sectional z-scores, monthly (§3a–b).
  3. Factor returns, estimated here, daily, by regressing each day's stock returns on the latest monthly exposures (§3c–e). This is the standard BARRA cross-sectional approach. The factor returns are an output of our regression, not a bought or downloaded series. The monthly-to-daily step is in §3c.
The only raw data we buy (all free) are 13F holdings, identity mappings, company fundamentals and daily prices. The factor structure is built from those, which is why §3 is the substance of the model.

Every input is free or public-domain, fetched over HTTP and cached to disk, so reruns are cheap and do not hit rate-limited APIs again. The four raw feeds below go into the builder. The fifth row, factor returns, is the estimated output from the callout above, listed here so all six frames sit in one place.

2 · Transformation register — positions & identity

  1. 13F parsing. Every information table for the CIK is parsed to (report date, filing date, CUSIP, $ value). Multiple lots or share classes of one issuer are summed per filing.
  2. Identity resolution. CUSIP to FIGI via OpenFIGI, ticker to CIK via SEC. All frames key on FIGI. CUSIP, ticker and CIK never leave the builder.
  3. Point-in-time weight overlay. Weights are normalised within each filing (Σw = 1). Each calendar month-end is then matched to the latest filing on or before it, so the book only counts once it has been filed. Names dropped from the newest filing expire with it, so no stale positions persist. Weights sum to exactly 1.0 on all 108 month-ends.

2·b · Estimation universe & its intersection with the 13F filings

The factor model is estimated cross-sectionally (§3), so each day it needs a population of names to regress over. That is the estimation universe. It is built in two parts: the whole 13F book plus a market-index seed. Across the sample Soros filed 52 quarterly 13F-HR reports holding a median of 166 cash-equity names each (2,461 distinct CUSIPs in total, options and bond lots dropped). Those resolve to 1067 names, and the universe keeps them all, so the book is always a subset of the universe. To make the cross-section a genuine market rather than one manager's holdings, the universe is then seeded with the S&P 500: its constituents are unioned in, adding 129 names not already in the book, for a total estimation universe of 1196. The result is a market cross-section that fully contains the book, which is what a Barra-style model needs.

How the populations nest. The 1196-name universe is the whole 13F book (1067 names) plus the index seed (+129 names not already held). 1188 of them have usable loadings and enter the daily regression. The held book is always a strict subset: 792 names are held at some point in the sample, and on any one date the active book is the names in the latest filing that are also in the universe (median 80, range 54–119). The point worth making: the regression that builds the factor returns (§3) runs over the full 1188-name market cross-section, not just the names Soros holds. So the factors describe a market, and index names Soros has never held still add breadth. The cube prices the full filed 13F book each quarter, and every §6 number reflects that.

Why this shape. Factor-return quality goes up with breadth, and the daily regression skips any date with fewer than 30 valid names, so a wide market cross-section beats the manager's holdings alone. That is why we seed an index. The only real cost is build time: each name needs cold-cache SEC, OpenFIGI and Stooq calls plus a fundamentals pull. Those are rate-limited, cached to disk, and run in parallel across worker threads, so build time grows with the universe. To go wider, point SEED_INDEX at a bigger benchmark (S&P 1500 or Russell 3000). The book stays a subset by construction.

3 · Transformation register — exposures, factor returns, specific risk

This is the heart of the model and the part with no external benchmark, so every stage is set out. Exposures, factor returns and specific risk all come out of one regression. The monthly exposure panel is the regression's right-hand side (the loadings), and the same regression produces the factor-return cache and the residual variances. They are never spliced from different sources. The flow runs in eight stages and moves the data from a monthly exposure cadence to a daily factor-return cadence. Stage (c) is the bridge.

monthly characteristic exposures
cross-sectional z-scores (a–b) ⟶ held fixed for the month
prior month-end loadings (c) ⟶ daily cross-sectional WLS
stock returns ~ lagged exposures (d–e) ⟶ daily factor returns
the scenario cache

How to read this. For each month-end we build a table: one row per stock, one column per metric (Size, Value, Momentum, Leverage and so on). Each cell is that stock's value for the month. Stack the monthly tables and you have a block with three axes: stock, metric, month. Read one stock down the months and that is its monthly history for a metric. Read one month across all stocks and that is the cross-section. Stage (a) fills the table from prices and fundamentals. Stage (b) scores each column, one month at a time. The point to be clear on: the exposure is not the raw value. It is a score for where the stock sits on that metric against the rest of the universe that month, in standard deviations. A Size score of +2 means the stock is two standard deviations bigger than the median name that month. That is what lets us compare Size against Value, and one month against the next, even though the raw numbers (log dollars for Size, a ratio for Value) are on different scales.

A worked example. Take one stock, one month, one metric. Say its raw Value (book equity / market cap) is 0.045 in June. To score it we look at every stock's Value that June, take the middle and the spread, and turn 0.045 into something like +0.7. That just says it is a bit cheaper than the typical name that month. We store +0.7 as the stock's Value loading for June. July runs the same way over July's stocks and gives the next point in the series. So the exposures we feed the model are just the scored version of the per-stock monthly metrics you asked about.

  1. (a) Descriptor construction, per name, on the monthly month-end calendar. Price-based (trailing daily prices): Beta = cov(stock, SPY) / var(SPY) over a 252-day window (needs ≥120 observations, else withheld); ResidVol = annualised σ of that regression's residual (×√252); Momentum = 12-1 total return, pricet−21 / pricet−252 − 1 (the most recent month is skipped); Liquidity = log of mean daily dollar-volume over the trailing 63 days. Fundamental-based (SEC XBRL, as-of joined on the filed date so nothing is known before it was reported): Size = log(mcap+1); NonLinSize = log(mcap+1)³; Value = book equity / mcap; EarnYield = net income / mcap; Leverage = assets / equity; Growth = period-over-period asset growth. Market cap = split-adjusted close × shares outstanding (DEI cover-page count). Market-cap floor: if a name's mcap falls below $10,000,000 that month we withhold its fundamental descriptors (set them missing, not zero). A corrupt share count would otherwise turn every ratio into raw dollars (§5).
    Two cadences in one monthly panel. The price descriptors are recomputed each month over a trailing daily window, so they move every month as new prices arrive. The fundamental descriptors only step when a new SEC filing lands (quarterly, as-of joined on the filing date) and stay flat between filings. So in a typical month a name's Beta, Momentum, ResidVol and Liquidity drift while its Size, Value, EarnYield, Leverage and Growth stay put. The panel refreshes price signals every month and fundamental signals every quarter.
  2. (b) Robust cross-sectional standardisation (winsorisation). Each descriptor is standardised within each month's cross-section, not across time. The transform is robust rather than the usual mean/σ. Centre by the median and scale by the MAD (median absolute deviation × 1.4826 for normal consistency), winsorise the score at ±3σ (a hard clip), then re-standardise the clipped column to mean 0, σ 1. If a cross-section has no spread (MAD ≈ 0) it carries no information, so we set the whole column missing for that month. Median/MAD centring is what makes the clip work: one wrong-units outlier cannot move the centre or the scale, so the ±3σ clip lands it at a finite, harmless value instead of letting it dominate the column. That is the failure the earlier quantile clip ran into (§5). NonLinSize is also orthogonalised to Size (regressed on Size, the residual kept and re-standardised), so the factor is the genuinely non-linear part of size, not a second copy of Size.
  3. (c) The monthly-to-daily bridge (Barra timing). Exposures are slow-moving, so we compute them monthly. Factor returns have to be daily for the tails to be real. Here is the bridge. Walk consecutive month-ends (d₀, d₁). For every trading day d in the window (d₀, d₁], hold the loadings fixed at their d₀ (prior month-end) values and let only the left-hand side, that day's stock returns, change. So one monthly exposure panel feeds about 21 daily regressions before the next panel takes over. This is standard Barra timing: returns lead, loadings lag. It turns ≈108 monthly snapshots into 2,204 daily factor-return observations, which is the difference between a 99% tail estimated from ~108 points and one estimated from thousands. The cost: loadings drift inside the month, and the last few days of a window regress on a month-old panel (§7).
  4. (d) Per-day universe and factor screening (removing degenerate factors). Before each day's regression we screen the design matrix:
    • Name screen. Keep names carrying at least 6 of the 10 loadings (the full price block plus some fundamentals), and zero-fill the rest. A missing standardised exposure is the market-average tilt, which is the Barra convention. Requiring complete rows here used to drop whole thin months below the name minimum.
    • Factor screen. Drop any factor column whose cross-sectional standard deviation that day is ≤ 0.05. A near-constant regressor is collinear with the intercept and makes the normal equations near-singular (the old Value/EarnYield blow-ups, §5). That factor simply does not exist for that day and contributes no return. The robust z-scoring in (b) should prevent this. The screen keeps the solver safe either way.
    • Minimums. Skip the day unless at least 30 names survive and the Size column is present (Size is also the WLS weight). Daily stock returns beyond ±50% are masked as data errors (split or adjclose glitches) rather than clipped, so a fake −90% never enters a regression.
  5. (e) The daily cross-sectional WLS regression. For each surviving day we regress the day's stock returns y on the screened exposure matrix X with a column of ones prepended. That intercept is the Market factor. It is weighted least squares with weight ∝ √mcap, so large names anchor the fit (implemented by multiplying both sides by mcap¼). The fitted coefficients are that day's factor returns Δfk, and the residuals are the name-specific returns. Output: 2,204 days × (10 style + Market) factor returns, the shared scenario cache.
  6. (f) Specific (idiosyncratic) risk. We square each day's regression residual and run it through an EWMA with a 63-trading-day half-life per name, giving a daily specific variance per (name, day). For the cube join we snapshot this at each calendar month-end (the model's join cadence). The specific block is strictly diagonal: one variance per name, no cross-name specific covariance, in line with the daily VaR horizon.
  7. (g) Market intercept as a leaf loading. After the regression we give every (date, name) a Market loading of exactly 1.0 in the exposure panel. This is the leaf form of the regression intercept. A fully-invested book (Σ weights = 1) then carries unit market exposure, so the directional market move flows through the scenario engine. We add it after stage (e) so the style factor returns are estimated on style exposures only and are not contaminated by the constant.
  8. (h) Scenario construction. One table keyed (ScenarioSet, Factor) → shock vector. HistFull is the full daily factor-return history. Evt:* is that history cut to a past event window. Hypo:* is single-day shocks of n×σ per factor (σ = that factor's daily vol). All three feed the same calculation, dPnL = Σk xk·Δfk. Only the source of the Δf vector changes.

5 · Data quality, controls & the shares-outstanding incident

A 30-check DQ suite runs after every rebuild: key uniqueness, referential integrity across all six frames, weight normalisation, calendar continuity, value ranges, and cross-frame coverage. Current status: 27 pass, 3 warn, 0 fail.

Incident, root-caused and defended. During validation the Value/EarnYield factor returns hit ±106 on 21 of 73 regression dates. The cause: us-gaap:CommonStockSharesOutstanding facts of 0 shares (CVNA) and 100 shares (CHWY) collapsed market cap, which turned ratio descriptors into raw dollars (109). Quantile winsorisation could not remove them. The outliers crushed every other name's z-score into a near-constant column, and the regression design matrix went near-singular (condition number 108). Four separate defences now stand: (1) shares come from the DEI cover-page tag; (2) a $10,000,000 market-cap floor withholds descriptors rather than emit wrong units; (3) median/MAD standardisation caps any surviving outlier at a harmless ±3σ; (4) the solver drops degenerate factor columns per date. 6 · Risk measures & current book snapshot (2024-12-31)

Definitions. All on the daily P&L vector of the current book (792 names over the sample, 105 held at the snapshot date):

  • VaR 99 = −P1(dPnL). The 1st percentile of daily scenario P&L (1-day horizon).
  • Worst loss = −min(dPnL). The single worst scenario day.
  • Specific vol = √(Σ w²·σ²specific). The diagonal block, currently 0.34% daily, with as-of specific variance available for 79% of held names.
  • Total VaR 99 = √(VaR² + (2.326·specific vol)²). The factor tail and an independent-normal idiosyncratic tail, combined.
  • VaR ladder 95 / 97.5 / 99 = −P5 / −P2.5 / −P1(dPnL). The 95/99 spread reads tail fatness; 97.5% is the FRTB regulatory point.
  • ES 97.5 / 99 = −mean of the worst 2.5% / 1% of days. Expected Shortfall (CVaR) — the average loss in the tail beyond VaR. Coherent / sub-additive where VaR is not, and the Basel FRTB replacement for VaR. Total ES 97.5 combines it with the idiosyncratic tail in quadrature, like Total VaR.
  • Risk HHI = Σname share², share = a name's fraction of book Total VaR. 1 / HHI ≈ the effective number of independent risk bets — the single-number concentration gauge a desk watches against a limit.
  • Marginal VaR / ES — a member's own P&L on the book's tail day(s). Additive: sums to the book VaR/ES, so it splits the tail across factors, sectors, names. Incremental VaR — the book VaR released by removing a member (recompute-without); diversification-aware and not additive, answering "what does cutting this release?".

7 · VaR backtest & model validation

The 13F book has no live daily P&L, so VaR is validated by a constant-portfolio backtest: the current book's exposures applied to the daily factor-return history, rolling a 250-day window to estimate VaR each day and counting exceptions where the realised day beat VaR. This validates the methodology, not a trading record.

  • Kupiec POF — a likelihood-ratio test that the observed exception rate matches the 1% claimed at 99%. χ²(1) at 95% = 3.841; above that, the calibration is rejected.
  • Basel traffic-light — green / amber / red from the binomial CDF of the exception count, generalising the 250-day/99% zones to any window.

Three estimators are available; the default was chosen by a sweep over this book:

8 · Risk tooling on the cube

Operational layers exposed in the dashboard, all reading the same cube and frames:

  • Desk limits (RAG) — book VaR / ES / Risk HHI and single-name / sector weight vs a desk limit set, red/amber/green with breach flags.
  • Data-quality panel — the §5 checks surfaced live against the served frames, plus the known stubs (Country, Unknown sector).
  • Risk-analyst commentary — an on-demand written read of any view's numbers (LLM, grounded strictly on the figures shown, no tool or data access), leading with any limit breach.
  • Risk trends — Scenario VaR / ES and Risk HHI, plus factor exposures, across the 2016–2024 calendar.
  • Stress test — custom one-day shocks (any per-factor σ → book P&L, same Σ xk·σk·volk engine as the Hypo sets) and reverse stress (the single-factor move that breaches a target loss, ranked by vulnerability).
  • Pre-trade / what-if — resize / drop / add a position and see book VaR, ES, Total VaR, Specific vol and Risk HHI before vs after; the risk math is reproduced in numpy so the "before" reconciles with the cube and the delta is the trade's effect.

9 · Limitations the numbers inherit

  • 13F is a quarterly, lagged, long-only disclosure. Intra-quarter trading, shorts, options and non-US listings are invisible. The book is the latest filed snapshot.
  • Estimation breadth. Factor-return quality and stability improve with the number of names in the daily regression (§2·b). A broader cross-section gives more reliable factors.
  • Index-seeded but US-large-cap. The estimation cross-section is the 13F book plus the S&P 500 (§2·b). That is a real market, but it is US large-cap-tilted, so small-caps and non-US names outside the book are absent. Point SEED_INDEX at a broader benchmark (S&P 1500 or Russell 3000) for a fuller market.
  • Sector is a free SIC→GICS proxy, Country is stubbed. Sector comes from each filer's SEC SIC code crosswalked to the 11 GICS sectors (CIK-keyed), so sector drill-downs work. A handful of BDCs and closed-end funds have no SEC SIC and fall back to "Unknown". Country is still a constant "US", pending a real domicile source.
  • Exposures update monthly. Daily factor returns are regressed on month-old loadings. This is standard Barra timing, but the loadings drift inside the month.
  • Specific-risk coverage is partial. Names outside the regression cross-section (incomplete descriptors) carry no specific variance that month and add zero to the diagonal block.
  • Free-data licensing. Stooq and Yahoo price data are free to use but not redistributable. This POC is for internal evaluation only.
